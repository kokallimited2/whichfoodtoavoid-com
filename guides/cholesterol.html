Foods to Avoid with Cholesterol: A UK Dietary Guide

Published 15 August 2026 • Nutrition Guide • For general information only

Raised cholesterol affects around six in ten UK adults — yet the dietary triggers often hide in plain sight in the weekly shop. The main foods to limit: fatty cuts of meat, full-fat dairy, butter, processed meats, commercial pastries, and tropical oils such as coconut and palm oil.

LDL vs HDL: What You Need to Know

Not all cholesterol is equal. LDL (low-density lipoprotein) deposits cholesterol in artery walls, raising your risk of heart disease and stroke. HDL (high-density lipoprotein) carries cholesterol back to the liver for removal. The dietary goal is to reduce LDL without suppressing HDL — and saturated fat is the primary nutritional driver of raised LDL.

Foods to Avoid with Cholesterol

FoodWhy it raises LDL
Fatty cuts of meat (lamb chops, pork belly, beef brisket)High in saturated fat — the primary dietary driver of raised LDL
Full-fat dairy (butter, cream, full-fat cheese, whole milk)The largest saturated fat source in the average UK diet
Processed meats (sausages, bacon, salami, chorizo)High saturated fat and sodium; associated with elevated cardiovascular risk
Hard margarine and lardConcentrated saturated fat; older hard margarines may contain residual trans fats
Commercial pastries, biscuits, and cakesOften made with palm oil or hydrogenated vegetable fat
Coconut oil and palm oilDespite health claims, both exceed butter in saturated fat content
Full-fat coconut milk and creamVery high in saturated fat; use in small quantities only

Note on trans fats: artificial trans fats (partially hydrogenated oils) are effectively removed from the UK food supply. Small natural amounts remain in some dairy and meat products but are far less harmful than the artificial form.

What You Can Eat Instead

  • Oily fish (salmon, mackerel, sardines) — omega-3 fatty acids support heart health
  • Oats and barley — beta-glucan soluble fibre actively lowers LDL by up to 10%
  • Unsalted nuts (almonds, walnuts, pecans) — unsaturated fats that improve the LDL/HDL ratio
  • Extra-virgin olive oil — replace butter and coconut oil; linked to lower cardiovascular risk in large trials
  • Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, kidney beans) — soluble fibre and plant protein with no saturated fat
  • Avocado — monounsaturated fat; a practical alternative to butter on toast
  • Skinless poultry and white fish — much lower in saturated fat than red or processed meat
  • Plant sterol and stanol foods (e.g., Benecol, Flora ProActiv) — clinical evidence supports LDL reduction of 7–10% with daily use

How to Make It Practical

Read the label. NHS guidance suggests no more than 20g saturated fat per day for women and 30g for men. On UK traffic-light labels, a red rating for saturated fat means more than 5g per 100g — avoid making those foods a daily habit.

Swap one thing at a time. Replace butter with an olive oil spread; switch whole milk to semi-skimmed or oat milk; choose grilled chicken over sausages. Compounding small changes is more sustainable than an overnight overhaul.

Change how you cook. Grill, bake, or steam instead of frying. When you do pan-fry, use a small amount of olive oil rather than butter or coconut oil.

Watch portions of healthy fats too. Nuts and olive oil are calorie-dense. A 30g handful of almonds is a heart-healthy snack; 200g is not.

Discuss next steps with your GP or a registered dietitian if LDL remains elevated after dietary changes. They can advise on whether plant sterols, further lifestyle measures, or medication is appropriate for you.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I eat eggs if I have high cholesterol?

For most people, the dietary cholesterol in eggs has a much smaller effect on blood LDL than saturated fat does. The NHS does not set a recommended limit on eggs for the general population. If you have familial hypercholesterolaemia or have been given specific advice by your GP, follow their guidance.

Is coconut oil heart-healthy?

No — despite widespread marketing claims, coconut oil is roughly 90% saturated fat, higher than butter. Current UK and European dietary guidelines do not support it as a heart-healthy cooking oil. Use extra-virgin olive oil as your everyday default.

Do I need to cut out all cheese?

Not entirely. Reduced-fat options such as cottage cheese, reduced-fat cheddar, feta, and ricotta are lower in saturated fat and can feature in a balanced diet. Full-fat hard cheeses are best treated as an occasional food rather than a daily staple.

How quickly can dietary changes lower my cholesterol?

Measurable LDL reductions can appear within 4–8 weeks of consistent changes — particularly when you cut saturated fat, add oats, and use plant sterols together. Ask your GP to arrange a follow-up blood test to track your progress.
