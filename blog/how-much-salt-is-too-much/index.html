How Much Salt Is Too Much? Hidden Sources and Easy Ways to Cut Back

Published 23 October 2026 • Healthy Eating • For general information only

The average American takes in about 3,400 mg of sodium every day. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans cap the recommendation at 2,300 mg. Most Americans eat far too much salt, and the excess rarely comes from the salt shaker. It hides in bread, deli meat, canned soup, and fast food.

What the Dietary Guidelines Say About Daily Sodium

The 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend no more than 2,300 mg of sodium per day for adults and children over age 14. One level teaspoon of table salt contains roughly 2,325 mg. The American Heart Association targets 1,500 mg for people with high blood pressure, though that level is difficult to hit without significant dietary changes.

The World Health Organization sets its own target below 2,000 mg per day. Every major nutrition body points in the same direction: most people in the US are eating well above the safe range.

Where Hidden Salt Actually Lives

Only about 11% of the sodium Americans consume comes from salt added at the table or during cooking. The CDC reports that roughly 70% comes from packaged and restaurant foods. Most of the problem is not in your spice cabinet.

Consider a few common grocery staples. One slice of Wonder Bread Classic White contains about 130 mg of sodium. Two slices for a sandwich adds 260 mg before any topping. Two thin slices of Oscar Mayer Deli Fresh Honey Turkey add roughly 470 mg. One cup of Campbell's Condensed Chicken Noodle Soup, prepared, carries approximately 890 mg, nearly 39% of the daily recommendation in a single bowl.

Cottage cheese surprises most people. A half-cup of Daisy Brand small curd contains about 400 mg. Canned olives, pickles, and jarred pasta sauces like Prego Traditional (about 480 mg per half cup) are quiet but consistent contributors throughout the day.

Restaurant Meals: Where Sodium Stacks Up Fast

Eating out adds sodium quickly. A McDonald's Big Mac contains 1,010 mg, close to half the daily limit before counting fries or a drink. A Subway 6-inch Italian BMT on Italian Herbs and Cheese bread runs about 1,180 mg.

Sit-down chains can be considerably worse. Chili's Chicken Fajitas for one exceed 4,300 mg in a single entree, nearly double the daily recommendation. One slice of Domino's Hand Tossed pepperoni pizza contains roughly 760 mg, and most people eat more than one slice.

None of this means restaurants are off-limits. Ordering grilled instead of breaded, skipping heavy cream sauces, or choosing a clear broth soup over a loaded entree can each trim several hundred milligrams per meal.

What Excess Sodium Does to the Body

High sodium intake raises blood pressure in many people. About 47% of American adults have hypertension, according to the CDC, and excess dietary sodium is one contributing factor. The body retains water to dilute sodium in the bloodstream, which increases blood volume and places more pressure on arterial walls over time.

Not everyone responds the same way. Research suggests that roughly 25 to 50% of adults with normal blood pressure, and up to 75% of those with hypertension, are "salt-sensitive," meaning their blood pressure rises noticeably with high intake. Because individual sensitivity is difficult to determine without clinical testing, USDA guidance applies broadly rather than selectively.

Short-term, a salty meal often causes noticeable bloating and temporary weight gain from retained fluid. These effects typically reverse within one to two days on a lower-sodium intake. Long-term, consistently high intake is associated with greater cardiovascular risk, which is why the Dietary Guidelines for Americans treat the 2,300 mg ceiling as a population-wide target.

Practical Ways to Cut Sodium Without Losing Flavor

Start with canned goods. Rinsing canned beans under cold water for 30 seconds removes 35 to 40% of their sodium content, according to USDA testing. Choosing "no salt added" canned tomatoes, such as Muir Glen Organic, then seasoning to taste gives you full control over the final sodium level.

Read labels carefully rather than trusting the front of the package. The FDA defines "low sodium" as 140 mg or less per serving. "Reduced sodium" means only 25% less than the original product, and the original may have been very high. Check the milligrams column directly.

In cooking, swap regular soy sauce for Kikkoman Less Sodium Soy Sauce, which contains about 575 mg per tablespoon versus 920 mg in the regular version. Lemon juice, white wine vinegar, and fresh herbs add real depth without adding sodium. Dash salt-free seasoning blends (formerly Mrs. Dash) offer 14 flavor varieties for roughly $3 to $5 per bottle at most Walmart or Target locations.

Cooking at home more often is probably the single biggest lever available. Restaurant and takeout meals average nearly twice the sodium of home-cooked equivalents. A grilled chicken breast seasoned with garlic and lemon contains fewer than 100 mg of sodium.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sea salt healthier than table salt?

No. Sea salt and table salt contain similar amounts of sodium by weight. Sea salt is less processed and may carry trace minerals, but those quantities are nutritionally insignificant. The sodium per teaspoon is essentially the same as ordinary iodized table salt.

Can you eat too little salt?

Yes, though it is uncommon in the US. Very low sodium intake below 500 mg per day can trigger hyponatremia, where sodium levels in the blood drop dangerously low. This rarely happens on a normal diet but can occur with extreme restriction combined with heavy exercise and sweating without electrolyte replacement.

Does drinking more water help flush out excess salt?

Partially. Staying well-hydrated helps the kidneys process and excrete excess sodium. It does not, however, counteract the blood pressure effects of a chronically high-sodium diet. The solution is reducing intake, not diluting it.

Are salt substitutes safe for everyone?

Not for everyone. Most salt substitutes, including Morton Salt Substitute, replace sodium chloride with potassium chloride. People with kidney disease or those taking ACE inhibitors or potassium-sparing diuretics should consult a doctor before using them. Excess potassium can be dangerous for those groups.
