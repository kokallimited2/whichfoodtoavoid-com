Are Greens Powders Worth It? What's Actually In Them

A greens powder is not vegetables. It is a powder made from dried and ground vegetables, grasses, algae and sometimes fruit, which you mix with water and drink. Whether that is worth £30–£60 a month depends entirely on what you are trying to replace, and for most people the answer is not what the marketing suggests.

What is actually in them

Most products blend a base of cereal grasses, wheatgrass, barley grass, alfalfa — with a small amount of algae such as spirulina and chlorella, then add flavourings, sweeteners and a "proprietary blend" of extras. That last term matters: it lets a manufacturer list ingredients without stating how much of each is present.

The practical result is that per-serving quantities of the headline ingredients are often in the hundreds of milligrams. A single portion of spinach is about 90g and carries several grams of fibre, plus iron, folate and vitamin K at doses no powder realistically matches.

Where they do help

Two situations are genuine. The first is people who eat almost no vegetables and want something better than nothing, a greens powder like TonicGreens legitimately fills a gap. The second is convenience: a powder travels in a suitcase where a fridge and a chopping board do not.

What they will not do is substitute for a diet. If you are eating five portions a day, a greens powder is an expensive addition rather than a fix.

How to judge one

Ignore the front label and read the supplement facts panel. Three things decide whether a product is serious:

Stated quantities. If an ingredient appears only inside a blend with no individual figure, assume it is token. A product confident in its formula states the milligrams.

A third-party test. Because these are unregulated supplements, a certificate from an independent lab is the only real evidence that what is on the label is in the tub. Look for the certificate, not a claim about one.

Fibre. If a product contains meaningful fibre it will say so. Fibre is the part of vegetables most people are short of, and it is also the part hardest to put in a powder, so silence on it is informative.

If your goal is not vegetables

Plenty of people buy greens powders hoping for energy or focus. If that is honestly the objective, a greens product is the wrong category, a nootropic such as The NeuroPrime targets that directly, and is a more sensible place to look. Equally, if you are eating low-carb and worried about nutrient coverage, a ketogenic-support formula like KetoSana addresses that approach rather than vegetable intake.

The short answer

Buy one if you genuinely do not eat vegetables and want a stopgap, choose a product that states its quantities, publishes a third-party certificate, and declares its fibre. Skip it if you already eat reasonably well and were hoping for an energy boost, because that is not what this category does.

Frequently asked questions

Do greens powders replace vegetables?

No. They contain a fraction of the fibre, and of the vitamins and minerals by weight, that whole vegetables provide. They are a supplement, not a substitute.

Are they regulated?

In the UK, food supplements fall under food law rather than medicines regulation, so claims are restricted and potency is not pre-approved. Independent lab testing is the practical check on what is actually in the tub.

When should you take one?

Timing matters far less than consistency and diet. Manufacturers vary, so follow the label rather than a general rule.

Is a cheap greens powder just as good?

Not necessarily either way. Price correlates weakly with quality here. Judge on stated quantities, third-party testing and declared fibre, the same three tests apply at any price.
