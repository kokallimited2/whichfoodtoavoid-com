About 58 percent of the calories the average American eats each day come from ultra-processed foods, according to a 2016 study in BMJ Open. That figure has not meaningfully changed. Ultra-processed foods are industrially manufactured products built primarily from refined ingredients and chemical additives that no home cook keeps in the pantry. Think Doritos. Oreo cookies. Oscar Mayer bologna. Kraft Macaroni & Cheese from the blue box.

What Makes a Food "Ultra-Processed"?

The most widely used classification system is NOVA, developed by researchers at the University of Sao Paulo. NOVA divides foods into four groups based on how much industrial processing they undergo. Group 4, ultra-processed, covers products formulated with substances rarely found outside a food factory: emulsifiers such as soy lecithin, synthetic color additives like Red 40, flavor enhancers such as disodium inosinate, and long lists of stabilizers, artificial sweeteners, and preservatives.

A bag of plain frozen peas is processed. Blanched, frozen, sealed in plastic. It still qualifies as Group 2 under NOVA because it is still, essentially, peas. A bag of Cheetos is Group 4. The difference is not cooking. It is industrial reformulation designed to create a product with no real whole-food equivalent.

Practical markers of ultra-processed products:

  • More than five ingredients on the label
  • Ingredients unavailable in a grocery store, such as maltodextrin or TBHQ
  • Products engineered for overconsumption through calibrated combinations of fat, sugar, and salt
  • Shelf life far longer than the equivalent whole food

How Much Are Americans Eating, and Why It Matters

A lot. Data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) shows ultra-processed foods supply roughly 67 percent of calories in children's diets and about 57 percent in adults. Adolescents aged 14 to 19 are the heaviest consumers in the country.

This matters for a concrete reason. The 2020-2025 Dietary Guidelines for Americans call for limiting "foods and beverages higher in added sugars, saturated fat, and sodium." Ultra-processed products tend to score high on all three. USDA guidance does not use the NOVA classification directly, but the dietary patterns it recommends, including the Healthy U.S.-Style Eating Pattern, are built around foods in NOVA Groups 1 through 3, not Group 4.

What the Research Actually Shows

The evidence base has grown fast since 2019. A randomized controlled trial by Kevin Hall and colleagues at the NIH found that participants eating an ultra-processed diet consumed about 500 more calories per day than those eating minimally processed foods, even when both diets were matched for total sugar, fat, fiber, and macronutrients. Body weight increased faster in the ultra-processed group, too.

Large observational studies consistently link higher ultra-processed food intake to elevated risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, depression, and all-cause mortality. Observational data cannot establish causation, and strong causal claims are harder to support than associations. What the data does support is that dietary patterns heavy in ultra-processed foods are associated with worse health outcomes across multiple populations and study designs.

One proposed mechanism involves food structure. A 2019 paper in Cell Metabolism found that ultra-processed foods are digested rapidly compared to their whole-food equivalents, which may alter satiety hormones like GLP-1 and peptide YY. The result: more calories consumed before fullness signals arrive.

Common Ultra-Processed Foods in the Average American Cart

Some of these are obvious. Others are sold as healthy choices.

  • Packaged bread: Most national supermarket loaves, including Wonder Bread and Nature's Own, contain dough conditioners, DATEM, and calcium propionate. None of those belong in home baking.
  • Flavored yogurt: Yoplait Original Strawberry contains modified corn starch and Red 40. Plain Greek yogurt from the same refrigerator case contains neither.
  • Breakfast cereal: Frosted Flakes, Cap'n Crunch, and even options marketed as nutritious, like Honey Nut Cheerios, contain added sugars and synthetic vitamins added back after processing strips the original nutrients out.
  • Frozen entrees: Hot Pockets, Stouffer's Macaroni & Cheese, and most microwaveable meals list 10 or more ingredients, several of which are industrial additives rather than food.
  • Protein bars: Many bars sold as health food, including certain Clif Bar and Quest Bar flavors, qualify as ultra-processed under NOVA due to isolated protein concentrates and added fibers like chicory inulin.
  • Flavored chips: Doritos Nacho Cheese lists 39 separate ingredients. Plain salted potato chips list three: potatoes, vegetable oil, salt.

Simple Swaps That Work on a Real Budget

You do not need a specialty grocery store or a $200 weekly food budget. Most of these cost the same per serving as the product they replace, sometimes less.

  • Bread: Look for loaves where the entire ingredient list fits in two short lines. Sourdough from a bakery counter often has four ingredients: flour, water, salt, starter. Most Kroger and Publix locations stock one for $4 to $6.
  • Yogurt: Buy plain Fage or Chobani whole-milk Greek yogurt (around $1.20 per serving at Walmart and Target) and add your own fruit or a teaspoon of honey. That cuts roughly 12 grams of added sugar per serving compared to Yoplait Original.
  • Hot breakfast: Store-brand rolled oats cost about $3.50 for 42 oz at Target or Aldi. Cooked with a banana and cinnamon, they take four minutes and have three natural ingredients. Far cheaper per bowl than any boxed cereal.
  • Frozen meals: A bag of Aldi frozen stir-fry vegetables ($2.29), combined with pre-cooked rotisserie chicken ($5.99 at most Costco and Sam's Club locations) and a low-sodium soy sauce packet, is ready in eight minutes and costs less per serving than a Stouffer's entree.
  • Snacks: An ounce of plain mixed nuts from a bulk bin costs roughly $0.50 and replaces a 160-calorie bag of flavored crackers with a shorter ingredient list and more protein per serving.

No single swap rewrites a diet overnight. Replacing one ultra-processed product per week, starting with whatever you eat most often, is a measurable starting point that compounds over months.

Frequently Asked Questions

Are all processed foods bad for you?

No. "Processed" covers a wide range. Canned tomatoes, frozen spinach, and pasteurized milk are all processed but remain nutritionally close to their whole-food origins. NOVA places these in Group 2 or Group 3. The concern is specific to Group 4, ultra-processed products reformulated with industrial additives and predominantly refined ingredients stripped of their original food structure.

Is whole wheat bread ultra-processed?

Most store-bought whole wheat bread qualifies as ultra-processed under NOVA because it contains dough conditioners, emulsifiers, and preservatives. That does not mean one slice of whole wheat toast will harm you. Overall dietary pattern matters more than any single food. If a less-processed option is the goal, look for bread with five ingredients or fewer, or buy from a bakery that bakes without conditioners.

Do ultra-processed foods cause cancer?

Some large observational studies, including a 2023 British Medical Journal meta-analysis covering nearly 10 million people, found associations between higher ultra-processed food intake and increased cancer risk. Observational data cannot establish causation, and overall diet quality and lifestyle confound results. It is accurate to say eating fewer ultra-processed foods aligns with USDA dietary guidance. Saying they directly cause cancer goes beyond what current evidence supports.

Can I eat ultra-processed food sometimes and still be healthy?

Yes. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans frame health around overall dietary patterns, not the elimination of any single food category. Regular consumption of ultra-processed foods as a staple is what most research flags as problematic, not an occasional bag of chips or a frozen burrito on a busy night. Reducing frequency and portion is a realistic starting point for most people.
