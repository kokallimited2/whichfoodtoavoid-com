Good Fats vs Bad Fats: Which Ones to Eat and Which to Limit

Fat was labeled the enemy for three decades. Then the science shifted. In the good fats vs bad fats debate, the current answer is clear: unsaturated fats support cardiovascular health, trans fats should be cut to near zero, and saturated fat needs to be limited, not eliminated entirely.

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025 dropped the total fat cap for most adults. What the guidelines kept: a clear limit on saturated fat at under 10% of daily calories, and a strong case against trans fat. How much total fat you eat matters far less than which type.

Why Fat Type Is the Real Question

All dietary fats are built from fatty acid chains, but their chemical structure determines how the body processes them. Unsaturated fats are liquid at room temperature. Saturated fats are solid. Trans fats are artificially altered through a process called hydrogenation, originally to extend shelf life.

The USDA and the American Heart Association link the ratio of these fats in your diet to LDL cholesterol levels, arterial inflammation, and long-term cardiovascular risk. Replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat consistently reduces that risk across large cohort studies. That is the core logic behind the good fats vs bad fats framework.

Unsaturated Fats: The Types to Eat More Of

Unsaturated fats split into two groups.

Monounsaturated fats (MUFAs) are found in olive oil, avocados, almonds, and peanut butter. A bottle of California Olive Ranch extra-virgin olive oil costs $9-$12 at Target and is a practical daily kitchen staple. One tablespoon delivers about 10 grams of MUFAs and zero trans fat.

Polyunsaturated fats (PUFAs) include omega-3 and omega-6 fatty acids. Omega-3s attract the most research attention for heart and brain health. Wild-caught salmon ($9-$12 per pound at Trader Joe's or Whole Foods), sardines, walnuts, and flaxseed are reliable sources. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend two seafood servings per week to support adequate omega-3 intake.

Omega-6 fatty acids are also PUFAs, present in sunflower oil and corn oil. The issue is not omega-6 itself, which is an essential nutrient, but the ratio in the American diet. Most Americans already consume far more omega-6 than omega-3. The fix is adding more omega-3 sources, not cutting omega-6.

Saturated Fat: Limit It, Don't Eliminate It

Saturated fat is solid at room temperature. Butter, coconut oil, full-fat dairy, beef tallow, and the fat marbled through a ribeye are all high in it. USDA guidance sets the ceiling at under 10% of total daily calories, which works out to about 22 grams on a 2,000-calorie diet.

In practice, a single tablespoon of butter has about 7 grams of saturated fat. A quarter-pound beef patty adds roughly 8 grams. Across a full day of eating, those figures accumulate faster than most people track.

None of that means a burger is permanently off the table. It means being aware of the cumulative daily total. Swapping butter for olive oil in everyday cooking produces measurable LDL reductions in clinical trials without requiring a complete dietary overhaul.

Coconut oil creates frequent confusion. It is approximately 82% saturated fat, higher than butter. Controlled studies show it raises LDL cholesterol. Using it occasionally in a specific recipe causes no measurable harm. Treating it as a health food, though, is not supported by the current evidence base or by USDA dietary guidance.

Trans Fat: The One to Cut Entirely

Artificial trans fat, produced through partial hydrogenation of vegetable oil, was banned by the FDA as a food additive in 2020. For most products at mainstream US retailers, this is no longer an active concern.

The catch: FDA rules allow a product with less than 0.5 grams of trans fat per serving to display "0g trans fat" on the Nutrition Facts label. Eat two or three servings and those fractions add up. The way to verify: check the ingredients list for the phrase "partially hydrogenated." If it appears, the product contains trans fat regardless of what the front-of-package label says.

Some microwave popcorn varieties, shelf-stable frostings, and certain flavored crackers still carry this. Reading the ingredients list takes five seconds and is the most reliable check available to a shopper.

Reading the Nutrition Label for Fat

The FDA-mandated Nutrition Facts panel lists total fat, saturated fat, and trans fat by weight per serving. Here is how to read it efficiently:

  • Total fat alone tells you very little. Look at the breakdown listed below it.
  • Saturated fat is the number to track against your daily budget, roughly 20-22 grams on a 2,000-calorie diet per USDA guidance.
  • Trans fat: Any non-zero figure is worth reducing. Cross-check the ingredients list for "partially hydrogenated."
  • Unsaturated fat is not required on the label, but you can estimate it: total fat minus saturated fat minus trans fat gives a working figure.

Six Swaps to Make at the Grocery Store

These substitutions are grounded in Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommendations and produce concrete changes in daily fat intake:

  • Use olive oil instead of butter when cooking vegetables on the stovetop. One tablespoon of butter has 7 grams of saturated fat; olive oil has about 2 grams.
  • Replace 80/20 ground beef with 93% lean ground beef or ground turkey breast for weeknight meals.
  • Swap chips fried in palm oil for a 1-oz serving of Blue Diamond almonds, available at most supermarkets for around $6 for a 6-oz bag.
  • Add one serving of canned fatty fish per week. Wild Planet sardines cost about $3.50 at Walmart and deliver roughly 1.5 grams of omega-3s per serving.
  • Switch from coconut oil to avocado oil for high-heat cooking. Avocado oil is about 70% monounsaturated fat and has a smoke point above 500°F.
  • Before adding snack crackers or microwave popcorn to your cart, scan the ingredient list for "partially hydrogenated oil."
Is butter bad for you?

Butter is not harmful in small amounts, but it is high in saturated fat, about 7 grams per tablespoon. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommend keeping saturated fat under 10% of daily calories, or roughly 22 grams on a 2,000-calorie diet. If you also eat red meat, full-fat cheese, and whole milk regularly, butter contributes to a saturated fat total that can push above that threshold. For everyday cooking, olive oil is a practical swap that lowers saturated fat intake without cutting fat altogether.

Is coconut oil a healthy fat?

Coconut oil is approximately 82% saturated fat, which is higher than butter. Controlled dietary studies consistently show it raises LDL cholesterol. Using it occasionally in a recipe does no measurable harm. Describing it as a health food does not align with the current evidence base or with USDA dietary guidance, which does not list it as a recommended fat source.

Are omega-6 fats bad for you?

No. Omega-6 fatty acids are essential nutrients the body cannot produce on its own. The concern in the American diet is the ratio of omega-6 to omega-3, which is heavily skewed toward omega-6. The practical solution is adding more omega-3 sources, such as fatty fish, walnuts, and flaxseed, not cutting sunflower or corn oil.

How much fat should I eat per day?

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans 2020-2025 recommend getting 20-35% of total calories from fat for most adults. On a 2,000-calorie diet, that is 44-78 grams of total fat per day. The more important figures: keep saturated fat below about 22 grams and trans fat as close to zero as possible. The source of the fat matters more than the total gram count.
