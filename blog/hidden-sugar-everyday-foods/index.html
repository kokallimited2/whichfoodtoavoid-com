The average American eats 17 teaspoons of added sugar a day. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans sets the limit at 10. Hidden sugar in foods like pasta sauce, salad dressing, flavored yogurt, and sliced bread accounts for most of that gap. You do not need to avoid every processed food. You need to know where sugar hides and how to read the one line on a nutrition label that tells you exactly how much is there.

How Sugar Gets Hidden in Plain Sight

Manufacturers list more than 60 names for sugar on ingredient labels. Maltodextrin, dextrose, cane juice, barley malt, turbinado, and corn syrup solids all count as added sugar. The FDA updated the Nutrition Facts label format in 2020 to require a dedicated Added Sugars row, expressed in grams and as a percentage of the 50-gram Daily Value (based on a 2,000-calorie diet). That one row is the fastest screening tool available.

Sugar in savory foods serves a specific purpose. It rounds out acidity, masks bitterness, and extends shelf life. A product can contain 10 grams of added sugar without tasting sweet at all. That functional role explains why sugar turns up in tomato sauce, hot dog buns, and fat-free salad dressing.

Breakfast Cereals and Granola Bars

Many Americans treat cereal as a neutral grain product. Quaker Granola Honey & Oats delivers 12 grams of added sugar in a 28-gram serving. That is 24% of the USDA daily limit before 9 a.m. Kellogg's Raisin Bran runs around 9 grams per cup. Flavored instant oatmeal packets commonly hit 10 to 13 grams each.

Granola bars sit in the same range. Clif Bar Chocolate Chip (68g) has 21 grams of added sugar. Kind Caramel Almond & Sea Salt has 5 grams, making it an outlier in a category where 8 to 12 grams is the norm. Plain rolled oats, by comparison, contain 0 grams of added sugar. A canister of Quaker Old Fashioned Oats costs roughly $4 to $5 at most US supermarkets and delivers zero added sugar per serving.

Condiments, Sauces, and Dressings

This category accounts for some of the most concentrated hidden sugar in the American diet. None of the following foods taste notably sweet.

  • Prego Traditional Pasta Sauce: 10 grams added sugar per half-cup serving (most Americans use a full cup)
  • Sweet Baby Ray's Original BBQ Sauce: 16 grams per 2 tablespoons
  • Kraft Original BBQ Sauce: 16 grams per 2 tablespoons
  • Heinz Ketchup: 4 grams per tablespoon
  • Ken's Thousand Island Dressing: 6 grams per 2 tablespoons

A dinner of pasta with Prego sauce, a side salad with Thousand Island dressing, and grilled chicken with ketchup can deliver 30 or more grams of added sugar. No dessert required. Gram counts here are drawn from 2024 to 2025 US product labels; manufacturers reformulate periodically, so verify against the current label before acting on specific figures.

Foods Sold as Healthy

Low-fat and fat-free labels reliably predict added sugar. When manufacturers remove fat, they typically compensate with sugar to restore palatability. Yoplait Original Strawberry Yogurt (6 oz) has 19 grams of total sugar. Most low-fat flavored yogurts fall in the 15 to 20 gram range per serving.

Bottled smoothies are a concentrated source. Naked Juice Green Machine (15.2 oz) contains 53 grams of total sugar. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans treat 100% fruit juice as equivalent to other sugar-dense beverages when consumed in excess, primarily because juice lacks the fiber that slows sugar absorption in whole fruit.

Kombucha surprises many shoppers. GT's Synergy Trilogy (16 oz) has 12 grams of sugar per bottle. Some brands halt fermentation early to keep the drink sweeter and more approachable, leaving more residual sugar than the health marketing implies.

Drinks Beyond Soda

Soda's sugar content is well-documented. Less discussed are the grams hiding in other popular beverages.

  • Starbucks Iced Caramel Macchiato, grande (16 oz, 2% milk): 33 grams of sugar
  • Gatorade Fruit Punch (20 oz): 34 grams
  • Vitamin Water Dragonfruit (20 oz): 27 grams
  • Ocean Spray Cranberry Cocktail (8 oz): 25 grams
  • Arizona Green Tea with Ginseng (23 oz can): 51 grams

Most flavored sparkling waters are safe. LaCroix and Waterloo list 0 grams of added sugar. Some store-brand sparkling water products do not. Reading the Added Sugars line takes under five seconds and removes the ambiguity entirely.

How to Read a Label in Under 30 Seconds

Start with the serving size. A pasta sauce listing half a cup as a serving when you use a full cup means every number on the label doubles. This step catches more hidden sugar than any other.

Next, find the Added Sugars row under Total Carbohydrate. A percentage Daily Value above 10% per serving (5 grams) means the product is a meaningful sugar source. Above 20% per serving, roughly 10 grams, qualifies as high-sugar under USDA guidance.

Then scan the ingredient list. Ingredients appear in descending order by weight. If any form of sugar appears in the first three ingredients, the product is heavily sweetened regardless of front-of-package claims. If three separate sugar aliases each rank low individually in one ingredient list, their combined contribution likely exceeds what a single entry would signal.

Aliases to recognize: dextrose, maltose, sucrose, fructose, barley malt extract, rice syrup, evaporated cane juice, turbinado, corn syrup solids, and anything ending in -ose. Knowing a dozen of these covers the vast majority of cases found on US supermarket shelves.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much added sugar per day is safe for adults?

The Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommends keeping added sugar below 10% of daily calories. On a 2,000-calorie diet, that is 50 grams, or about 12 teaspoons. The American Heart Association sets a stricter target: 25 grams per day for women and 36 grams for men.

Does 'no added sugar' mean a food has no sugar?

No. The claim means no sugar was added during manufacturing. Natural sugars from fruit, dairy, or vegetables can still be present. A 12-oz glass of unsweetened orange juice contains roughly 26 grams of naturally occurring sugar with zero added sugar on the label.

Are honey and maple syrup better than table sugar?

The body processes glucose and fructose from any source through the same metabolic pathways. Honey and maple syrup contain trace minerals, but at the quantities used in food, the amounts are nutritionally negligible. The FDA counts both as added sugars on the Nutrition Facts label. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans do not distinguish them from refined white sugar for the purpose of daily limits.

What are the most common code names for sugar on US labels?

More than 60 terms function as sugar under FDA labeling rules. Common examples in US packaged foods include dextrose, maltose, sucrose, fructose, barley malt, rice syrup, evaporated cane juice, turbinado, corn syrup solids, and anything ending in -ose. When several of these appear in a single ingredient list, the actual added sugar content is almost always higher than a quick glance suggests.
