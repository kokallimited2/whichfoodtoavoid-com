More than half of the calories the average UK adult eats every day come from ultra-processed foods — yet most people have never heard the term. Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) are industrially manufactured products loaded with additives you would never find in a home kitchen, and a growing body of research links high consumption to obesity, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular disease.

What Exactly Makes a Food 'Ultra-Processed'?

The most widely used classification system is the NOVA framework, developed by nutrition researchers at the University of São Paulo. It divides all foods into four groups based on the extent of industrial processing. Ultra-processed foods sit in Group 4 — the most heavily altered.

What sets them apart is not just high levels of salt, sugar, or fat. The defining feature is the presence of industrial additives that serve no nutritional purpose: emulsifiers such as lecithin (E322) or carrageenan (E407), flavour enhancers like monosodium glutamate, artificial sweeteners, modified starches, and hydrogenated vegetable oils. These ingredients exist to extend shelf life, improve mouthfeel, or make food hyperpalatable — not to nourish you.

A useful rule of thumb: if the ingredient list contains more than five items, or includes anything you could not buy in a supermarket yourself, the product is likely ultra-processed.

The Health Evidence: What Studies Actually Show

The research linking UPFs to poor health is predominantly observational — meaning scientists have tracked large populations over time rather than conducting randomised controlled trials. This means cause and effect cannot be stated with certainty. That said, the associations are consistent and sizeable.

A major 2019 cohort study published in The BMJ found that every 10% increase in UPF consumption was associated with a 12% higher risk of overall cancer. Further studies have linked high UPF intake to greater risks of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, depression, and premature death. A 2023 umbrella review in The BMJ — analysing 45 meta-analyses covering nearly 10 million participants — found convincing associations between UPF consumption and heart disease, type 2 diabetes, anxiety, and early death.

Researchers point to several possible mechanisms: the high calorie density of many UPFs, disruption to gut microbiome diversity, rapid digestion causing blood sugar spikes, and potentially direct effects of certain additives. It is most likely a combination of factors rather than any single cause.

UK Foods You Might Not Expect to Be Ultra-Processed

The obvious culprits — multipacks of crisps, fizzy drinks, and fast food — most people already treat as occasional extras. The trickier category is everyday staples that appear healthy but still qualify as UPFs:

  • Sliced supermarket bread — including many wholemeal loaves from Warburtons and Hovis. Look for emulsifiers (E471, E472) and dough conditioners on the ingredient list.
  • Flavoured yoghurts — Müller Corner, Yoplait Fruit on the Bottom, and similar products. Plain full-fat Greek yoghurt is a straightforward swap.
  • Breakfast cereals — even options marketed as high-fibre, such as many bran flakes and granola bars, often contain added flavourings and high levels of sugar.
  • Plant-based meat alternatives — Quorn pieces and many supermarket own-brand vegan burgers contain long lists of binding agents, flavour extracts, and stabilisers.
  • Low-fat ready meals — the “healthy eating” ranges at Tesco, Sainsbury’s, and M&S frequently use thickeners and flavour enhancers to compensate for reduced fat.
  • Flavoured oat and nut milks — plain unsweetened versions are generally fine; vanilla or barista editions often contain added gums and sweeteners.

This does not mean all of these foods are equally harmful. Overall diet quality and context matter enormously. But it does illustrate that “ultra-processed” is not a synonym for “obvious junk food.”

Simple Swaps to Try This Week

Reducing UPF intake does not require overhauling your entire diet. Small, consistent substitutions tend to stick far better than dramatic changes.

  • Bread: Choose a sourdough from a local bakery, or look for supermarket loaves with short ingredient lists — flour, water, yeast, salt. Many Lidl and Aldi in-store bakery loaves qualify.
  • Breakfast: Swap branded cereal for plain porridge oats. A bowl of Quaker Original Oats with banana and a handful of frozen berries takes under five minutes and typically costs less per serving.
  • Snacks: Replace crisps with unsalted mixed nuts, rice cakes with peanut butter, or fresh fruit. Apple slices with a small piece of cheddar are genuinely satisfying.
  • Yoghurt: Buy plain full-fat Greek yoghurt — Fage, Yeo Valley, or a supermarket own-brand — and stir in fresh or frozen fruit yourself.
  • Convenience meals: Batch-cook a simple chilli, dhal, or pasta sauce at the weekend. Freezing portions means you have a quick home-cooked meal ready on busy evenings.
  • Drinks: Replace diet fizzy drinks with sparkling water and a slice of lemon or lime. The fizz delivers a similar sensory hit without the artificial sweeteners.

Aim for progress, not perfection. Eating an ultra-processed food occasionally, within an otherwise balanced diet, is very different from making UPFs the foundation of every meal.

What NHS Guidance Currently Says

The NHS does not yet use the NOVA classification in its official dietary advice. Its Eatwell Guide focuses on food groups and nutrient balance rather than degree of processing. However, NHS guidance already steers people away from the most problematic UPFs indirectly: it recommends limiting foods high in saturated fat, added sugar, and salt, and advises against regularly eating high-calorie packaged snacks and takeaways.

Several public health organisations — including Action on Sugar and the British Nutrition Foundation — have called for greater policy attention to ultra-processing. The government-commissioned National Food Strategy (2021) highlighted the dominance of UPFs in the UK diet as a significant public health concern, and interest from policymakers has continued to grow since.

In practical terms, the most NHS-aligned approach is to base meals on vegetables, wholegrains, legumes, fish, and lean protein; treat heavily processed packaged foods as occasional additions rather than daily staples; and cook from fresh ingredients when you realistically can.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is sliced bread always ultra-processed?

Not always. A loaf made solely from flour, water, yeast, and salt is not ultra-processed. The problem is that most supermarket sliced breads — including many wholemeal varieties — contain emulsifiers, preservatives, and dough conditioners. Read the ingredient list: if it runs beyond four or five items, check what those extras are.

Are all ultra-processed foods equally harmful?

No. The NOVA Group 4 category is broad, and individual products vary widely in their nutritional profiles. A flavoured yoghurt and a mechanically separated chicken nugget are both technically UPFs, but nutritionally they are very different. The research shows population-level associations with high overall UPF consumption — not that every product in this category carries identical risk.

Does “natural” or “no artificial flavours” mean a food isn't ultra-processed?

No. These are unregulated marketing claims with no legal definition in the UK. A product can carry both labels and still contain a long list of industrial additives such as modified starches, emulsifiers, or natural flavour extracts. The ingredient list is the only reliable guide.

Do I need to give up ultra-processed foods entirely?

The evidence does not suggest you need to. The studies find meaningful associations at high consumption levels — diets where UPFs account for the majority of daily calories. Reducing your overall intake and replacing even a portion of UPFs with minimally processed alternatives is a worthwhile step, without requiring perfection or eliminating every convenience food from your life.
